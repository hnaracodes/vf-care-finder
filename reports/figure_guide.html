CMU Datathon, Fall 2026 · what the cleaned data lets us claim

For emergency obstetric care, find the districts that look cut off in this data, then use the survey to tell a real gap from a blind spot.

We started with a broad plan: three services, and models meant to prove that access drives outcomes. The data narrowed it. The facility table is a sample of facilities that have websites: 88% private, 4.7% public, while public hospitals deliver about 74% of facility births. So distance "in the data" can only tell us where the data sees little. The government survey (NFHS-5) is the reality check. In a district that looks far from care, are babies still born in facilities? If they are, care exists that the data cannot see: send data collectors. If they are not, the gap is real: send care.

Snapshot 16:15 ET, Nov 21, corrected 16:45. Grades come from the regex baseline (recipe v0.1). Neither model is built yet; anything said about them is a plan. An independent audit is on its second round, so numbers can still move.

How the goal narrowed after cleaning

One service carries the story

Only obstetrics has a survey outcome to check against. Dialysis becomes a need-versus-distance map. ICU drops out of the split.

The evidence bar is grade 2

4,577 facilities claim obstetrics, 444 name a C-section, 43 also name blood and anaesthesia. Grade 3 is too thin to map; grade 1 is only a tag.

"Far in the data" is about the data

The table is a web-visibility sample, and facilities with more scraped text show more evidence. Distance is never proof that care is absent.

Model B is a plan, not a result

Visible access barely tracks facility births (rank correlation about −0.2). A design agent's quick, unreviewed preview found that adding access does not improve the model's predictions. If that holds once the model is built, Model B only sets an expected birth rate from development, and we say plainly that access added nothing.

Only stable districts go on screen

69 of 137 data deserts and 46 of 98 care deserts keep their label under all 16 reasonable threshold choices. The video features those.

Data quality is a finding too

Misplaced facilities, post offices stacked on placeholder points, and chain-wide bed counts go back to Virtue Foundation and Databricks as results.

1. Exploring the raw data

Exploration comes before any model: what does one row mean, how full is each column, where will a join or a number mislead us. Each chart answers one question. Every number was computed by scripts/eda_raw_figures.py.

Figure 1 · eda_01

Who is in the facility table: 88% private

How to read it

Top bar: the whole table split by who runs each facility. Lower bars: one per facility type, coloured the same way. Numbers are facility counts.

What we did

Dropped 88 junk rows and 11 exact duplicates, turned the text "null" into a real missing value, merged "government" into public, then counted type by operator.

What it shows

8,835 private, 467 public, 687 unknown. 440 of the 467 public facilities are hospitals; only 26 clinics are public.

So what

Public primary care is almost invisible here, so any access number built on this table understates public care. That is the data-desert half of the project.

Figure 2 · eda_02

Facilities sit in cities; C-section evidence is far from most villages

New chart type: a cumulative curve. Pick a distance on the bottom axis; the height of a line there is the share of post offices that have that kind of facility within that distance. A line further right means care is farther away. The dot is the median: half the post offices are closer, half farther.

How to read it

Left: grey dots are 151,010 post offices, our stand-in for where people live. Blue dots are facilities. Right: one curve per kind of facility.

What we did

For every post office, found the nearest facility by straight-line (great-circle) distance with a nearest-neighbour search, three times: any facility, a public one, one whose facts mention a C-section.

What it shows

Median distance: 18 km to any facility, 44 km to a public one, 54 km to one that mentions a C-section.

Careful

The flat grey streak across the south is 695 post offices sharing one fake latitude (15.5934). Distances are straight lines, and post offices are not weighted by population.

Figure 3 · eda_03

201 of 755 districts have no facility in the data

How to read it

Left: how many districts have 0, 1, 2 and so on facilities. Right: the 20 districts with the most.

What we did

One PIN code can cover several districts, so a plain join would count a facility twice. We gave each PIN its most common district first, then counted, keeping empty districts as zeros.

What it shows

201 districts at zero, 101 with exactly one. The top 20 (big cities like Pune and Ahmedabad) hold 38% of all facilities.

So what

A count per district measures web presence, not care. This is why the project uses distance from every post office instead of counts.

Figure 4 · eda_04

Claims outnumber evidence about ten to one for obstetrics

How to read it

Three groups, one per service. Light bar: lists the specialty (a claim). Middle: the facts name the procedure. Dark: the procedure plus the pieces it needs.

What we did

Read each facility's specialty list, then searched its facts with the keyword patterns in the recipe doc ("caesarean", "blood bank", and so on).

What it shows

Obstetrics 4,577 claim, 444 name a C-section, 43 corroborated. Dialysis 2,153, 1,036, 587. ICU is the odd one: more facilities mention an ICU than list the specialty.

So what

The map uses the grade, not the specialty tag. With 43 corroborated facilities, grade 2 is the working bar for obstetrics.

Figure 5 · eda_05

A third of "capability" facts are not about care

How to read it

Left: how many facts each facility has. Right: each bar is one fact field, split into rough categories.

What we did

Counted facts per facility, then put each fact in the first keyword group it matched (ratings, photos, staff, web, location), else "mostly clinical".

What it shows

Median 35 facts. 34% of capability facts are noise like "Rated 4.3 on JustDial". Procedure facts are 98% clinical. The spike at 150 is a hard cap of 50 facts per field.

So what

The fact tagger (Model A) must learn to ignore noise. Facilities at the cap may have lost evidence.

Figure 6 · eda_06

Location is complete; size is mostly missing

How to read it

Each bar is the share of facilities with any value in that column. Blue are fields an analyst needs; grey are web fields.

What we did

Counted values that are not the text "null" (pandas thinks "null" is a real value, so the usual check would say 100% everywhere).

What it shows

Coordinates, type and PIN are about 99% full. Beds 25%, doctors 36%.

Careful

Present is not plausible: bed counts reach 200,000. That is why the patient swarm does not model hospital capacity.

Figure 7 · eda_07

Facts come from Facebook and listing sites, some unrelated

How to read it

Left: websites cited by the most facilities, coloured by kind. Right: how many different websites each facility's facts draw on.

What we did

Pulled the website name out of every source URL and counted each site once per facility.

What it shows

Facebook 8,840, JustDial 5,425. Real estate, insurance and map sites appear for 39% of facilities. Only 57% of facilities with an official site cite it.

So what

"Many sources" is weak corroboration. A later check found facilities with 8 or more sites are the most likely to be placed in the wrong spot (26% sit over 50 km from their PIN).

Figure 8 · eda_08

Most districts deliver over 90% of births in facilities; state matters most

How to read it

Left: districts by their facility-birth rate, plus a bar showing how much of the variation is between states versus within them. Right: one row per state, dots are its districts, the black tick is the state median.

What we did

Parsed every survey cell (flagging "*" as missing and bracketed values as low-sample), then split the variation into a between-state and a within-state part.

What it shows

Median 92.2%, piled up near 100%. 64% of the variation is between states. Nagaland is lowest (median 39%).

So what

The outcome has a ceiling and is driven by state. Models are tested by holding out whole states, so they cannot just memorise them.

Figure 9 · eda_09

Development predicts facility births; care-use columns would cheat

New idea: a rank correlation (Spearman) runs from −1 to +1. +1 means districts higher on one measure are always higher on the other; 0 means no link; negative means one goes up as the other goes down. It uses ranks, so the pile-up near 100% does not distort it.

How to read it

Top: each dot is a district; the black line is the middle value within each tenth of the x axis. Bottom: each candidate feature's correlation with facility births.

What it shows

Clean cooking fuel +0.59, electricity +0.53, women with 10+ years of school +0.52, share of population under 15 −0.72. These are correlations across districts, not causes.

Why so many measures about women?

NFHS is mainly a survey of women aged 15 to 49, so its schooling and marriage questions are asked of women. Here they only stand in for how developed a district is. The model makes no claim about why any woman does or does not give birth in a facility.

Grey bars

Skilled birth attendance (+0.90) and antenatal visits measure the same pregnancies as the target. Using them would "predict" births from a near-copy of births. That is called leakage.

Not every blue bar is used

The current plan, not yet built, uses 9 development measures (electricity, water, sanitation, clean fuel, women's literacy, women with 10+ years of school, marriage before 18, population under 15, child stunting) plus 2 for rurality and district size. Insurance and "females ever schooled" are left out: insurance mostly identifies the state. The grey ones are excluded on purpose.

Figure 10 · eda_10

The blind spot: 4.7% of the data is public, 74% of facility births are

How to read it

Left: three shares side by side. Right: each dot is a state. Across: public share of facilities in our data. Up: public share of facility births in the survey. On the thin diagonal line they would be equal; every state sits far above it.

What we did

Divided public-facility births by all facility births in the survey, averaged over districts, and compared with the public share of rows in the facility table.

What it shows

Nationally 4.7% versus 73.5%. Even Kerala, the most private state in the survey, is 36.5% versus 6.5%.

So what

The strongest single argument that many "gaps" in this data are blind spots, not missing care.

Figure 11 · eda_11

The survey columns we need are clean

How to read it

Left: for each group of survey indicators, the share of cells that are plain numbers, low-sample estimates (in brackets), or suppressed (*). Right: the columns this project uses.

What it shows

Child-health columns are often suppressed. The plan's columns are clean except the private-facility C-section rate (122 low-sample, 150 suppressed).

So what

Facility births is a safe target. A "*" is always treated as missing, never as zero.

Figure 12 · eda_12

Public facilities are much less visible online

How to read it

Three charts, private (blue) next to public (orange). Bars are shares within each group, so the small public group can be compared fairly.

What it shows

Median followers: 285 private, 36 public. Most public pages carry a 2025 update date, likely government portals.

So what

Web popularity would push public facilities down, so followers stay out of any trust score.

2. Grading facilities and measuring distance

Each fact gets tags, the tags give each facility a grade from 0 to 3, and then we measure how far every post office is from the nearest facility at each grade.

Figure 13 · wp3_regex_gold_scores

C-section and blood tags hold up; "ob_delivery" is wrong two times in three on real data

New idea: precision asks "when the tagger says yes, how often is it right?" Recall asks "of the facts that truly have this tag, how many did it catch?" F1 combines the two into one score from 0 to 1.

How to read it

One row per tag, worst at the top. Blue dot is precision, orange is recall; the grey line is the gap between them. These are scores on the labelled sample, which flatters the tagger (next note).

What we did

An AI agent, not a person, labelled 429 distinct facts one at a time. The sample was built so every tag has at least 25 true examples, so 93% of its rows carry a tag, against about 9% of real facts.

What it shows

On the sample, average F1 0.90. Re-weighted to how often each kind of fact really occurs, C-section, blood and operating theatre still score 0.97 to 1.00. But "ob_delivery" precision falls from 0.63 to 0.33 (the bare word "obstetric" triggers it), and ventilator recall falls from 0.77 to 0.59.

Careful

No human has checked these labels, and the same kind of model wrote the tagging rules, so they are not ground truth. Next: Hruday labels 100 facts without seeing the agent's tags, and we report how often the two agree.

Figure 14 · wp5_01

Raising the evidence bar moves obstetric care from 24 km to 166 km away

How to read it

The same cumulative curves as figure 2, one panel per service, one line per evidence grade. Lighter is "claims it", darker is "proven".

What we did

For 145,715 post offices (dropping 5,874 placed over 200 km from their own district), found the nearest facility at each grade or better.

What it shows

Obstetrics medians 24, 60 and 166 km. For dialysis and ICU the lines sit close together: evidence changes less there.

So what

For obstetrics, evidence is the story. That is why it is the flagship service.

Figure 15 · wp5_02

Half of post offices are over 60 km from evidenced obstetric care

How to read it

Each dot is a post office, coloured by distance to the nearest grade 2 facility: grey within 60 km, light blue 60 to 120, dark over 120. The grey blobs are the areas around evidenced facilities.

What it shows

Obstetrics: 50% of post offices beyond 60 km, with dark patches across central and eastern India and the Northeast. Dialysis 32%, ICU 20%.

So what

This is a still preview of the interactive map. Dark means "no evidence nearby in this data", not "no care".

3. The split: care desert or data desert

Each district gets an access reading (median distance from its post offices to grade 2 obstetric care) and an outcome reading (survey facility births). Crossing the two gives the four groups at the top of this page.

Figure 16 · wp6_01

137 districts look far but still deliver most births in facilities

New chart type: a log scale on the bottom axis. Each step is a multiple (2, 5, 10, 20, 50 km) rather than an equal amount, so districts at 3 km and at 300 km both fit and stay readable.

How to read it

Each dot is a district. Right means farther from evidenced care; up means more facility births. The vertical line is the farthest third (90.2 km); the horizontal line is the lowest third (87.3%). Circles mark C-section rates under 5%, a sign of no surgical access.

What we did

Split both axes at thirds and labelled each corner.

What it shows

Served 335, data desert 137 (blue, top right), cost or quality 135, care desert 98 (bottom right). Most circled districts are care deserts.

Careful

The cuts are relative, so the labels overclaim. "Poor" means the bottom third, below 87.3% facility births: 40 of the 98 "care deserts" still deliver 80 to 87% of births in facilities. "Cost or quality" is a guess, since no cost data went into the rule. Absolute cuts are proposed (under 80% care desert, 90% or more data desert, 80 to 90% left unlabelled), and the next chart shows how much the choice matters.

Figure 17 · wp6_02

About half of each desert label survives any reasonable cut

How to read it

Across: where the cut goes (the share of districts called "poor"). Coloured lines: districts in each group at that cut. Black line: districts whose label differs from our chosen split (the vertical line at a third).

What we did

Moved one cut at a time, and also tried 16 combinations (median, third, quarter, or fixed 60 km and 80%).

What it shows

Counts swing a lot. 69 of 137 data deserts and 46 of 98 care deserts keep their label under all 16.

So what

The video should name only those stable districts. This is a robustness check: a finding that flips with a small choice is not a finding.

Figure 18 · wp6_03

Data deserts deliver more births in public facilities

How to read it

Each dot is a district, grouped by label. The black cross is the group median with a 95% range from resampling whole states.

What we did

Tested the data-desert idea with a column we did not use to make the split: the share of births in public facilities.

What it shows

Data deserts 77% of all births in public facilities, served districts 67%. Within the same state the gap is smaller (+3.3 points) but unlikely to be chance (p = 0.006). In care deserts, 91% of the few facility births are public: private care is simply absent.

Careful

Public share also rises in rural areas, so this is consistent with hidden public care, not proof of it.

Figure 19 · wp6_04

Deserts cluster by state

How to read it

One bar per state, split into its districts' labels.

What it shows

Data deserts: Odisha 17, Madhya Pradesh 15, Telangana 14, Karnataka and Rajasthan 13. Cost or quality dominates Uttar Pradesh (43) and Bihar (30): listed private care nearby, but fewer facility births. All 11 Nagaland districts are care deserts.

Careful

Cuts are national, so a state with few web-listed facilities looks far everywhere. Model B compares districts within their state to correct for this.

Figure 20 · wp6_05

Dialysis: 59 districts with high need and far from evidenced care

How to read it

Each dot is a district. Right is farther from grade 2 dialysis; up is more diabetes and high blood pressure. Purple dots are in the top third of both.

What it shows

59 such districts. The far-right dots are island districts over 1,000 km from the mainland.

Careful

Need is not an outcome, so this cannot tell a care gap from a data gap. Dialysis stays a secondary story.
